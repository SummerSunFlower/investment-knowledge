Episode Summary

The host opens by sharing her lifelong struggle with anxiety, tracing it back to a panic attack at age eight. She describes how anxiety once felt like a constant companion — sometimes even a strange comfort — but ultimately held her back from joyful experiences.

After years of trial and error, she discovered that fighting anxiety head-on rarely works. Instead, she learned to "beat it at its own game" — using the same irrationality and imagination that anxiety uses against us to work in our favour. The episode presents four practical strategies that anyone can try immediately.

💡

The Four Strategies at a Glance

1

Relabel Anxiety as Excitement

Anxiety and excitement trigger the same physiological arousal. The only difference is how we label the feeling. Telling yourself "I'm excited" shifts the emotional valence from negative to positive.

"This is excitement about how this is going to turn out, I'm excited to see how I manage this."
2

Fight Unknowns with Knowns

Anxious thoughts often start with "what if." Counter them by listing concrete things you do know — your resilience, your track record, your support system.

"You do know that you are capable. You know that you've survived every situation you've been in before."
3

Personify Your Anxiety

Give your anxiety a harmless identity — a cartoon character, a silly name. This creates distance between you and the feeling, reminding you that you are not your anxiety.

"It's just a little kid playing dress up who's trying to scare you."
4

Channel It into Creativity

Anxious energy needs somewhere to go. Redirect it into painting, writing, knitting, building — anything creative. Studies show even 10 minutes of daily creativity significantly reduces anxiety.

"Anxiety is just energy, and energy doesn't disappear — it needs a place to go."

Intro

Welcome & The Host's Personal Story

Hello everybody, welcome back to the show. New listeners, old listeners — wherever you are in the world, it is so great to have you here for another episode as we break down the psychology of your twenties.

Anxiety feels like such a curse. I'm just going to come out and say it. I think a lot of us have probably wished for a day where we weren't anxious — just one day. Or we've considered what our lives would look like if we weren't held back by these hypothetical fears: what may or may not happen, what others might think, what disasters await, what terrible things could go wrong. It is a huge burden at times, even if it comes and goes in waves.

Me and my anxiety — my pal — we go way back. I remember distinctly having my first ever panic attack about the movie The Neverending Story when I was eight years old at a school movie night. And since then, I've always had these moments of extreme panic and anxiety — sometimes lasting for months, or just a baseline of nervousness for as long as I can remember.

I've even worried about the fact that I'm not anxious about anything at times, which is absolutely bizarre. I began to realize that my anxiety was almost comforting in a strange way — it provided me with an illusion of control and security. But actually, although it was making me feel like I could anticipate every single problem, that wasn't the case. Things happened that I never could have predicted. And it was taking away from a lot of joyful experiences — I would just sit with my anxious thoughts and try to problem-solve them, which I think is something we've all done.

I would try to think my way through a completely irrational scenario — and that just never works. Because even on the rare occasion I got through one hypothetical, my brain would present me with a bigger and trickier one. It felt never-ending.

Then a few years back, I started doing things differently. I stopped letting anxiety be the loudest voice in the room. I started finding ways to beat it at its own game — combining what I knew scientifically and psychologically about how anxiety operates, with mind games, tricks, and strategies discovered through trial and error. And it really helped me shift my thinking from "anxiety is a burden" to "anxiety is something I can make work for me."

That's exactly what I want to talk about today: four ways I've been able to make anxiety work for me — even in really tough, panicky times. These strategies have been my greatest tools. I hope you can try them all today.

Foundation

What Anxiety Actually Is — A Refresher

Having a refresher on what anxiety actually is might sound simple, but when we're wrapped up in fear and panic, we think our anxiety means more than it does. We need to be grounded in the facts.

When you're operating from a place of survival and pure fear, your ability to remember valuable information becomes clouded. We think our anxiety is much more valid than it is — that we should believe every single thought that passes through our brains. But here's the thing:

Anxiety's job is to be irrational. That is literally its job description. It presents us with the worst-case scenarios. It catastrophizes. It projects. It does not aim to see situations realistically.

Why? Because anxiety is your body and brain trying to protect you against a perceived threat. It exaggerates outcomes to either motivate you or push you to problem-solve in advance. Think of anxiety like your mind giving you a practice exam — but all the questions are impossible scenarios you'd never actually encounter in real life. And all the while, your anxiety is convincing you: "This is the real deal. Take this seriously."

Take feeling anxious about the future. What you're really experiencing is a fear of uncertainty. There's a lot you don't know. Our anxiety rushes to fill that space with worst-case scenarios: "If you can handle this, if you have a plan, then we'll be fine."

But here's the thing — those scenarios rarely, if ever, come true. The scenario where you lose everything, where everyone secretly dislikes you — that isn't going to happen. Your brain is serving up scenarios to give you a rigorous mental and physical workout, so that if they were to happen, you'd already be prepared.

When we acknowledge this — that our brain is trying to protect us but doing a terrible job of it — we see our situation more clearly. My opinion about a situation is just an opinion. Nothing more, nothing less. There's very little evidence for most of what I'm thinking. And I simply cannot tell the future.

Of course, anxiety often comes with very real physical symptoms: shaking, rapid heartbeat, shortness of breath, trembling, nausea, sweating, or a sense of dissociation. These feelings are very real, even if the thoughts driving them aren't. I'm not going to sell you snake oil and promise I can make that go away immediately. But I do want to share four ways to make it work for you.

Tip 1

Relabel Anxiety as Excitement

The first way I've stopped fighting my anxiety and made it work for me is tricking myself into believing that whenever I feel anxiety, it's actually excitement — choosing to label the sensations differently from how my mind wants to label them.

If my anxious mind wants to label a situation as a threat when it isn't — if it wants to lie to me — well, I can lie right back.

Here's an unusual perk: anxiety and excitement are actually incredibly similar. They activate the same arousal pathways in our body. A really interesting article from Harvard Business School, titled "Get Excited: Reappraising Anxiety as Excitement," explains how both states are rooted in uncertainty and involve high levels of bodily arousal — a similar physical response to a stimulus — and a sense of losing control.

When you're excited, you feel jittery, nervous, your heart races, your thoughts swirl — that's very similar to an anxious state. The only difference is what psychologists call valence. Anxiety has a negative valence — it makes us scared and uncomfortable. Excitement has a positive valence — it makes us feel attracted to and energized by a situation.

So, tap into that positive emotional valence. If the only thing that's different is how we interpret the feeling, then tell yourself: "Actually, I'm excited by this. I'm excited to see how I manage this. I'm excited to have this human experience."

I used this a lot when overcoming my fear of flying. During takeoff — my most panicked moment — I'd just keep repeating: "This is an excited feeling. These are butterflies, not adrenaline. This is enthusiasm." The same Harvard paper found that this technique can meaningfully improve performance, especially in situations involving social anxiety, public speaking, and specific phobias. And it works much better than trying to go from anxious to calm, because that gap is huge. Going from anxious to excited is far more achievable — they're already on the same end of the spectrum.

Tip 2

Fight Unknowns with Knowns

The easiest way to tell if something is an anxious thought is when it starts with "What if…" — "What if the world collapses?" "What if my worst fear comes true?" "What if I never achieve what I want?"

A true, immediate danger wouldn't require that "what if" prefix — because it would be happening right now. The moment you find yourself spiraling, overthinking, and your thoughts all start with "what if" — that is anxiety. That is not reality. It's a thought, not a fact.

Here's how you make this work for you. What you're really fearing is the unknown. We often have five, six, or seven different hypotheticals swirling around simultaneously, each feeling equally scary — and we forget that they can't all possibly come true. We feel bombarded by a cumulative worry. That's one of the first signs anxiety is being irrational.

Fight those unknowns with knowns. Yes, you don't know how the future will unfold. But you do know a lot of things:

  • You know you are capable.
  • You know you have survived every situation you've been in before.
  • You know this is just anxiety.
  • You know your fears rarely come true.
  • You know you are not alone.
  • You know many others have been through this and come out the other side.

There is one uncertainty — what's going to happen next. But there are many certainties. Focus on those, and watch the certainties stack up in your favour.

I also use a visualization exercise: I imagine holding my certainties as big golden orbs in my hands, and I mentally throw them at my fears — visualized as shadowy figures — and watch the certainties crash through, making the shadow smaller and smaller until it's gone. These certainties really do pierce through the uncertainty, both in the visualization and in real life.

The reason I find this so effective is that it gives me the opportunity to remember I'm capable no matter what. Anxiety's job is to torment us — but we rarely get the chance to notice that what we were fearing never came true. This strategy lets you use that realization to reinforce your confidence — you trust yourself, not your anxiety. And remember: a lot of people go through life never getting tested this way. You are someone who faces the deepest fears your mind can conjure and still gets through it. That makes you incredibly strong.

Tip 3

Personify Your Anxiety as Something Non-Threatening

The third way I make anxiety work for me is by personifying my anxiety as something harmless.

My mum actually inspired this. She'd watched me battle fairly existential fears for years, and one day she said: "The fears you have now are no different from when you were a kid and scared of the boogeyman — it's all smoke."

Thinking about my anxiety as a tangible thing that was only scary in my imagination turned a lot around for me. So now, I personify my anxiety as Boo from Monsters, Inc. — the little girl in the tiny monster costume who jumps out and goes "Boo!" and scares Sully. But really? It's just a little kid. Harmless. Just playing.

And that's how I imagine my anxiety trying to scare me — like a little kid playing a game. I play along: "Oh, I'm so terrified! How scary!" But really, I can see it's just this little harmless being that cannot actually hurt me. Your anxiety can't rip off your limbs. It can't pull your hair. It cannot do anything to you externally. It is a feeling, and one that originated from you.

Giving it a name, a little costume, an identity — that lets us remember what we're really dealing with: a boogeyman. A kid playing dress-up. This also means you can speak to your anxiety like a person. You're asking it to leave. You're in control. You can tease it, joke with it, tell it to chill out.

One of my friends calls her anxiety "Brian" — brain with the I and A swapped. She'll say: "Brian is really overthinking this today — can you just reassure me?" or "Brian is holding us up, sorry I'm running late." This separation of you and your anxiety into a character reminds you: you are not your anxiety. You're just a person doing the best you can with the hand you've been dealt — even if that hand includes a little friend who likes to jump out and scare you.

Tip 4

Channel Anxious Energy into Creativity

My final tip is to take the tension, the hopelessness, the nervousness — and channel it into something creative. That energy has to go somewhere.

Yes, it can be expressed through tears, panic, or muscle tension. But it can also be expressed through creation. I'm not talking about productivity — I'm not saying: use your nervous energy to power through your to-do list. I'm saying: be creative, for the sake of making something beautiful.

Anxiety puts us in a state of helplessness, scarcity, and stuckness. We feel like life is meaningless, like everything could be taken away from us. Creativity is the exact opposite. Painting, buying four-dollar clay from the craft store, knitting, building Lego, scrapbooking, writing poetry, keeping a diary — anything that shows abundance, healing, and control. You can put beautiful things back into the world.

I often cite this paper, so regular listeners will recognize it — a 2021 study on the positive effects of creativity looked at over five hundred adults asked to engage in a small creative task each day for less than ten minutes. After two weeks, their subjective wellbeing was measured. Those given the direction to be creative were significantly happier, and the biggest area of improvement was — you guessed it — reduced anxiety.

In my mind, anxiety is just energy, and energy doesn't disappear — it needs a place to go. When you start to master your anxiety, you can channel it in the direction you choose. Creative expression is one of the easiest starting points. You take the energy that is entirely yours and use it to create, expand, and build — rather than letting your anxiety use it to convince you that nothing changes or your fears come true.

You know, a friend once said to me: "You're just very creative." And she's right. So take that creativity. You can either use it to cook up the most terrifying hypothetical scenarios — or you can use it to create something beautiful that adds to your life. Your choice.

Outro

Recap & Closing Words

So, as a recap — here are the four ways to make anxiety work for you:

  1. Relabel it as excitement — when you feel panic, tell yourself it's excitement.
  2. Fight unknowns with knowns — keep a running list of what you're certain of to overwhelm what you're not.
  3. Give your anxiety a name and a character — visualize your worries, make something harmless out of them.
  4. Be creative — when anxiety tells you to be small, build something instead.

I really hope that if you're going through an anxious period, this helps. The biggest comfort for me has been knowing I'm not alone — and you're definitely not alone either. I'm feeling it pretty intensely right now myself, so we're in the same boat.

I promise: it gets better. Your body cannot stay in this state forever — biologically, the stress response must pass. Hold on. Try some of these tips. This is just your imagination working overtime — which is a beautiful thing sometimes, but sometimes less than helpful.

I hope you find some peace in your day today. Until next time, stay safe. 💛
